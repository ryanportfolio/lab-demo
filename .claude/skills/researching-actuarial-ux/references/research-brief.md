# Independent actuarial UX research brief

## Objective

Investigate what makes incumbent insurance actuarial modeling products and the workflows around them clunky, stale, unintuitive, fragmented, slow, or difficult to trust. Look beyond explicit complaints: experienced users may normalize repeated labor, workarounds, and missing capabilities. Establish what Prediction Lab currently offers, which problems it appears to target, and which important problems remain uncertain.

This brief defines questions, not answers. Do not import conclusions from previous Prediction Lab research.

## Required questions

1. Who performs the work, what decision are they making, and what artifacts cross each workflow stage?
2. Where do users lose time, context, confidence, control, or continuity?
3. What compensating behaviors reveal latent needs even when users do not complain?
4. Which difficulties come from interaction design, product architecture, organizational process, regulation, integration, or statistical complexity?
5. How are charts, exact values, model diagnostics, assumptions, uncertainty, and business impact explored and preserved?
6. How do teams compare versions, remember failed work, collaborate, review, approve, reproduce, file, deploy, and later explain a decision?
7. What do current first-party sources establish about Prediction Lab's audience, product promise, shipped capabilities, AI roles, trust posture, deployment options, and direction?
8. Where do source types agree, conflict, remain silent, or show selection bias?
9. Which product opportunities are supported by evidence, and which are still hypotheses requiring observation or interviews?

## Research lenses

Cover the full problem or accept one caller-assigned lens:

- End-to-end actuarial work and handoffs
- Product interaction and learnability
- Data visualization and evidence exploration
- Versioning, collaboration, review, governance, and auditability
- Data preparation, compute, integration, deployment, and migration
- AI delegation, transparency, permissions, refusal, and human judgment
- Adoption, training, specialist dependence, procurement, and change management

An assigned lens changes emphasis, not evidence standards.

## Source classes

Seek independent classes rather than a raw source quota:

- Direct practitioner or user evidence
- Product interfaces, manuals, help centers, training, support, release notes, and demonstrations
- Professional bodies, conference material, research, regulatory guidance, and consulting studies
- Implementation artifacts such as job descriptions, templates, workflow diagrams, migration guidance, integrations, and required exports
- Vendor positioning and customer stories, explicitly labeled for commercial bias

Prefer primary sources for product capabilities. Prefer practitioner or independent evidence for pain and outcomes. Trace syndicated articles and customer stories to their underlying source so duplication is visible.

## Independence contract

During a blind run, do not open previous reports, synthesized findings, research-wisdom files, or prior agents' notes. Do not ask another researcher what they found. Record the model, date, scope, assigned lens, queries, and known contamination. Repeated independent discovery is useful evidence only when the runs did not share conclusions and did not merely repeat one underlying source.

## Research conduct

- Search for observable behavior and artifacts, not only opinions.
- Date current claims and note product/version context.
- Keep quotations short; preserve meaning through paraphrase and citation.
- Record failed searches and inaccessible evidence when they create a meaningful blind spot.
- Treat UI or workflow inference as inference even when it feels obvious.
- Avoid designing the interface while still collecting evidence.
